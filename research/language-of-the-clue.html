Language of the Clue: Cognitive Load in Visual Search

I'm bilingual in English and Turkish. While reading Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller, Ayres & Kalyuga, 2011) at the suggestion of my mentor at the Northwestern Hybrid Intelligence lab, I kept wondering: if the note on an image is in my weaker language, does decoding it take working memory that I would otherwise use to search the image? I built a platform to test that.

Status: designed and built, not yet run. The study is awaiting mentor review and ethics (IRB) approval. No participant data has been collected, and the live version runs in demo mode — nothing recorded there is research data.

Try the demo →
You keep going until you feel exhausted (60 minutes at most). Demo mode: answers are marked as test data and never analyzed.

The questions

  1. Language. Does a clue in your less comfortable language slow down visual search?
  2. The invisible gorilla. When your attention is on one job, does a weaker-language clue make you more likely to miss something unexpected? (In a famous 2013 study, 83% of radiologists missed a gorilla placed in a lung scan.)
  3. Fatigue. People keep going until they declare exhaustion. When does performance start to slip, how tired do they say they feel along the way, and does the weaker-language cost grow as they tire?
  4. Who. How do these differ across age, education and image-reading expertise — from high school students to PhDs to radiologists?

How it works

1. Surprise imageFind a small ring on a scan. A gorilla is also hidden there and never mentioned. Then: “Did you notice anything unusual?” and “Did you see a gorilla?” Half of each group gets this clue in their comfortable language, half in their weaker one.
2. RoundsIdentical rounds of seven tasks, one item of each in each language: find a named object in a busy scene; spot what is out of the ordinary in a scan; follow written moves on a dice grid; repeat a sequence of lit squares forward or backward; answer a question about a short passage; sort a card by a written rule; and remember words between quick sums. Each person sees half of everything in each language, so everyone is their own comparison.
3. Until exhaustionPeople continue until they press “I’m exhausted” (60 minutes at most), rating how mentally tired they feel after every round. A word-memory check comes last.
MeasuresSpeed and accuracy per task, a 1–9 mental-effort rating per item, 0–100 fatigue ratings, time to exhaustion, word retention, and the surprise questions
GroupsHigh school students, adults, PhD students and professors, physicians, radiologists
AnalysisMixed-effects models on response time and effort; gorilla notice rates with a logistic model; a piecewise time model to locate the start of decay; time to exhaustion as survival data; retention hit and false-alarm rates — checked on simulated data
Languages30 languages; any pair can be tested. Translations await review by native speakers before real data collection.

The pictures

What I'm careful about

  • No results claimed. Until real, approved data exist, this is a design and a working platform — nothing more.
  • Not a “bilinguals are smarter” claim. Earlier “bilingual advantage” findings often failed to replicate. This compares the same person under two clue languages.
  • Ethics first. The platform refuses to treat anything as real data until IRB approval is configured, and it stores no names, emails or IP addresses.
